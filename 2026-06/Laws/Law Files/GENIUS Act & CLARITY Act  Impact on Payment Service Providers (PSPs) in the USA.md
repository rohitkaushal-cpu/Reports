# GENIUS Act & CLARITY Act: Impact on Payment Service Providers (PSPs) in the USA

## Executive Summary

The GENIUS Act, signed into law on **July 18, 2025**, is the most consequential piece of payments legislation in decades. For the first time, it creates a federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins in the United States — governing who can issue them, on what terms, and under whose oversight. Its companion legislation, the **CLARITY Act**, which passed the House 294-134 and cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026, extends this regulatory architecture to the full spectrum of digital assets.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

Together, these acts are forcing every category of payment service provider — banks, card networks, fintechs, remittance firms, corporate treasury platforms, and crypto-native companies — to re-evaluate their business models, compliance infrastructure, and competitive positioning.

**The core economic reality:** Stablecoin payment volume reached **$390 billion in genuine real-economy payments** in 2025, growing 55% year-over-year, with B2B settlement accounting for 58% of that volume — up 733% in a single year. Simultaneously, stablecoin-linked card spending grew 673% year-over-year, reaching $4.5 billion in 2025. The GENIUS Act acts as an accelerant to this trend, removing the regulatory ambiguity that was the primary barrier to institutional adoption.[^5][^6][^7]

## 1. The GENIUS Act: What PSPs Are Actually Required to Do

The GENIUS Act creates a **mandatory compliance architecture** for any entity touching payment stablecoins in the US. The key requirements are:

### Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers (PPSIs) — Three Types
1. **Federally chartered PPSIs** — subsidiaries of banks or uninsured national banks, supervised by the OCC
2. **State-qualified PPSIs** — operating under state regimes certified as "substantially similar" to the GENIUS Act, limited to under $10 billion in circulation
3. **Foreign PPSIs (FPSIs)** — foreign-domiciled issuers meeting U.S. requirements who meet a qualifying threshold[^8][^3]

From **July 18, 2028**, it will be illegal for any Digital Asset Service Provider (DASP) to offer, sell, or make available a payment stablecoin to U.S. persons unless it was issued by a PPSI or qualifying FPSI. This is the single most consequential provision for non-bank PSPs.[^9][^8]

### Core Compliance Requirements for Issuers

| Requirement | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **1:1 Reserve Backing** | 100% backing with high-quality liquid assets — cash, short-term U.S. Treasuries | [^1][^10] |
| **Reserve Segregation** | Reserves must be separated from other issuer assets with diversification requirements | [^11] |
| **Monthly Attestation** | Independent audit by Big Four accounting firms; CEO/CFO certifications | [^12][^11] |
| **Weekly Reporting** | Issuance, redemption, trading volume, and reserve assets reported weekly | [^11] |
| **Quarterly Financials** | Detailed financial statements and compliance metrics each quarter | [^11] |
| **AML/BSA Compliance** | Issuers classified as "financial institutions" under Bank Secrecy Act; AML program required within 180 days of licensing | [^2][^10] |
| **Sanctions Compliance** | Must block sanctioned addresses at the smart contract level; token freezing capability required | [^3] |
| **No Interest to Holders** | Issuers prohibited from paying interest or yield; non-issuers (e.g., custodians, exchanges) may offer "rewards" | [^13][^14] |
| **Anti-Tying Provision** | Issuers cannot condition stablecoin services on customers purchasing other services | [^14] |
| **Bankruptcy Protection** | GENIUS Act amended federal bankruptcy laws to protect stablecoin holders in issuer failure | [^15] |

## 2. Impact by PSP Category

### 2A. Card Networks — Visa & Mastercard

**Threat Level: High | Timeline: Medium-Term**

Stablecoin payment volume grew 88% year-over-year to **$10.7 trillion**, now equivalent to **81% of Visa's total payment volume** and **6.3x PayPal's** — with an 85% CAGR vs. Visa's 7%. The two networks collectively earned approximately **$95 billion in swipe fees** in their latest fiscal years.[^16][^17]

The structural threat is clear: stablecoins bypass card rails entirely. A transaction on-chain has near-zero interchange cost versus 6–9 cents per transaction on card rails. Visa earned 6.6 cents in processing fees per transaction last quarter, down from nearly 9 cents a decade ago. Major retailers including **Walmart and Amazon** are reportedly exploring launching their own stablecoins.[^18][^16]

Visa and Mastercard shares fell **5% each** on June 18, 2025 when the U.S. Senate endorsed stablecoin legislation, and subsequently dropped **19–23%** on broader stablecoin fears.[^19][^18]

However, both companies are adapting: Visa and Mastercard are becoming "stablecoin-friendly" by integrating stablecoin settlement layers, while maintaining their network trust infrastructure. Mastercard explicitly stated the GENIUS Act "opens the door to a future filled with more innovation".[^20][^21]

**Most exposed revenue stream:** Cross-border payments and FX conversion, where stablecoins provide near-instant, low-cost settlement vs. multi-day card processing.[^18]

***

### 2B. Banks (as PSPs and Stablecoin Issuers)

**Threat Level: High on Deposits | Opportunity: New Revenue Streams**

The GENIUS Act creates a **federally sanctioned entry point for banks** to issue stablecoins through OCC-supervised subsidiaries — but also subjects them to stringent obligations exceeding traditional deposit structures.[^3][^22]

Deloitte's 2026 Banking Outlook estimates **more than $1 trillion in bank deposits could be at risk** from stablecoin competition, flowing primarily from three buckets:
1. Corporate working capital held in low-yield transaction accounts
2. Retail transaction balances for 24/7 P2P and commerce use
3. Cross-border settlement float trapped in nostro/vostro networks[^23]

The Federal Reserve modeled a $1 trillion deposit drain reducing bank lending by **$600 billion to $1.26 trillion**; even a $500 billion drain could contract loans by $190–$408 billion. Kroll Financial Intelligence identified **$6.6 trillion in demand deposits** facing partial disintermediation pressure.[^24][^25]

The GENIUS Act's prohibition on issuers paying interest was intended to protect deposits — but the "rewards loophole" (non-issuers can offer rewards) is being exploited by affiliated entities, making the protection largely ineffective according to a June 2026 Forbes analysis.[^24]

Grant Thornton's assessment identifies three strategic pathways for banks:[^22]
- **Launch own stablecoins** using existing regulatory relationships
- **Partner with established issuers** as custodian and reserve holder
- **Defend deposits through competitive rates** and relationship banking

Interchange fee income — over **$12.5 billion in non-interest income in Q2 2025 alone** (nearly 15% of total non-interest income) — is specifically highlighted as vulnerable to structural erosion.[^25]

***

### 2C. Non-Bank Fintechs & Digital Wallet Providers (PayPal, Stripe, Block, etc.)

**Threat/Opportunity Level: Dual-Edged | Compliance Burden: Significant**

Non-bank entities can become PPSIs under the GENIUS Act, but must meet bank-equivalent compliance standards: 1:1 reserves, AML programs, federal/state licensing, and OCC oversight. This dramatically raises fixed costs and **favors larger, well-capitalized entities** over smaller fintechs.[^26][^22][^24]

Key dynamics for this segment:

- **PayPal** launched PYUSD stablecoin ahead of the GENIUS Act and is well-positioned to become a PPSI, embedding stablecoins directly into its payment ecosystem[^21]
- **Stripe** has integrated stablecoin payments for merchants. The FTC sent warning letters to Stripe, PayPal, Visa, and Mastercard in March 2026 regarding non-discrimination in service access[^27]
- The CLARITY Act introduces a **provisional registration regime for crypto exchanges, brokers, and dealers** with disclosure, recordkeeping, and membership requirements[^28][^26]
- Non-bank DASPs face a **hard deadline of July 18, 2028**: after that date, offering any non-PPSI-issued payment stablecoin to U.S. persons is illegal[^8][^9]

***

### 2D. Cross-Border Payments & Remittance Providers (Western Union, Remitly, Wise, MoneyGram, etc.)

**Opportunity Level: Very High | Urgency: Immediate**

Cross-border payments are the single largest opportunity for stablecoin disruption. Remittance fees globally remain above **6%**, creating enormous economic incentive for blockchain-based settlement. The GENIUS Act's passage has directly triggered product launches across the remittance sector:[^29][^30][^31]

- **Western Union's** digital remittances are contributing over **25% of consumer money transfer revenue** for the first time in 2025[^31]
- **Remitly and Wise** report significantly higher digital revenues compared to traditional retail-focused competitors[^31]
- **MoneyGram, Nuvei, Corpay, and Visa** have all announced new stablecoin-based cross-border products[^30]
- A **1% excise tax on cash-funded remittances** introduced in 2025 is accelerating the shift from cash-based to digital stablecoin remittance corridors[^30][^31]

The BIS Paper No. 167 on Cross-Border Payment Technologies explicitly identifies the GENIUS Act as one of the regulatory frameworks enabling stablecoins as cross-border payment instruments. The market opportunity for stablecoin-based cross-border payments has been estimated at **$16.5 trillion**.[^32][^33]

BCG identifies B2B cross-border payments as the primary beachhead for stablecoins today, with B2B volumes at approximately **40% of stablecoin payment activity and growing at ~65% annually**.[^34]

***

### 2E. Corporate Treasury & B2B Payment Platforms

**Opportunity Level: Highest Near-Term | Timeline: Immediate**

This is where the most tangible disruption is already happening. B2B stablecoin payments hit **$226 billion** in 2025 — 58% of all real-economy stablecoin volume — driven by:
- Supply chain payments and cross-border supplier settlements
- Corporate treasury management and payroll disbursements
- ERP-integrated programmable payment workflows[^6][^7]

McKinsey outlines three strategic paths for financial institutions in this space:[^35]
1. Join a **multi-bank consortium stablecoin** (like JPMorgan's Kinexys)
2. **Partner with a global issuer** (USDC, USDT, PYUSD)
3. Accept **deposit flight** to stablecoin alternatives

The BRG analysis specifically highlights that the GENIUS Act creates a **"payments charter"** — effectively a new type of banking license centered purely on payments — which could be transformational for corporate treasury platforms seeking to operate outside traditional banking infrastructure.[^15]

***

### 2F. Crypto-Native Companies & DASPs (Coinbase, Circle, Tether, etc.)

**Opportunity Level: High for Compliant Players | Existential for Non-Compliant**

The GENIUS Act is existential for this category. Companies like **Circle (USDC)** and **Tether (USDT)** must obtain PPSI status or qualify as FPSIs to continue serving U.S. persons after July 2028. Circle's soaring valuation following the GENIUS Act passage was directly cited as a sign that the market views regulated stablecoin issuance as a major opportunity.[^9][^16][^8]

Tether's USAT stablecoin released its first GENIUS Act-compliant **reserve attestation by Deloitte** on January 31, 2026 — the first practical demonstration of the Act's operational requirements. The attestation confirmed 17.5 million tokens outstanding backed by $17.6 million in reserves, including $13.95 million in Treasury-collateralized reverse repos.[^12]

## 4. Compliance Roadmap for PSPs: What Oliver Wyman and PwC Prescribe

Both Oliver Wyman and PwC have published detailed compliance frameworks for PSPs navigating the GENIUS Act:[^11][^43]

**Immediate Actions (0–6 months post-enactment)**
- Conduct a PPSI eligibility assessment — bank subsidiary, state-qualified, or foreign issuer path
- Map all existing stablecoin-related activities against the DASP prohibition timeline (July 2028 deadline)
- Engage primary federal regulator (OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, or NCUA depending on entity type) proactively
- Assess existing AML/KYC program adequacy against Bank Secrecy Act standards

**Near-Term Infrastructure Build (6–18 months)**
- Implement 1:1 reserve management infrastructure with segregated accounts
- Establish weekly reporting pipeline for issuance, redemption, volume, and reserves
- Contract with a Big Four accounting firm for monthly attestation certifications
- Build sanctions screening at the smart contract level (token blocking/freezing capability)

**Strategic Positioning (18 months+)**
- Decide on issuer vs. custodian vs. distribution-only role in the stablecoin ecosystem
- Evaluate participation in multi-bank consortium stablecoin networks (JPMorgan Kinexys, SWIFT tokenization, etc.)
- Develop tokenized deposit or stablecoin product in response to competitive pressure on deposits and payment fees

## 5. The CLARITY Act: Additional Obligations for Broader Digital Asset PSPs

The CLARITY Act, which cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026 by a 15-9 vote, extends the regulatory perimeter beyond stablecoins:[^4]

- **CFTC jurisdiction** over digital commodities (Bitcoin, Ethereum, and most non-stablecoin crypto assets)[^26]
- **SEC jurisdiction** over tokens constituting investment contracts and capital-raising activities[^26]
- **Provisional registration regime** for crypto exchanges, brokers, and dealers — requiring disclosure, recordkeeping, and membership compliance during transition[^28]
- **$75 million exemption** for digital commodity issuers offering assets with specific disclosure conditions[^44]
- **Customer fund segregation** requirement across all registered digital asset firms[^26]
- **DeFi safe harbor** exempting non-custodial protocol participants (developers, validators) from registration[^28]

For PSPs that operate any kind of digital asset trading, custody, or intermediary function, the CLARITY Act creates a mandatory registration timeline and disclosure framework running parallel to GENIUS Act compliance obligations.[^44][^26]

## 6. Key Open Policy Issues That Will Shape PSP Strategy

The Brookings Institution identifies four unresolved issues that will determine how disruptive the GENIUS Act ultimately is for PSPs:[^45][^46]

1. **The Interest/Rewards Loophole** — The prohibition on issuers paying yield does not prevent affiliated non-issuers from offering "rewards." Banks are lobbying Congress to close this; crypto firms are lobbying to preserve it. The outcome determines whether stablecoins become competitive deposit substitutes[^13][^24]

2. **Par Convertibility and the "Singleness of Money"** — Ensuring $1 of stablecoin always equals $1 of bank deposit requires robust redemption infrastructure that many smaller issuers have not yet built[^45]

3. **Illicit Finance Controls** — The Treasury's March 2026 Congressional Report identified blockchain analytics and AI as the primary tools for GENIUS Act AML enforcement, representing a new compliance technology requirement for all PSPs[^47]

4. **Operational Resilience Standards** — Fraud, cybertheft, and system outages pose systemic risks in a stablecoin-based payment system that regulators have not yet fully addressed[^45]

## 7. Competitive Landscape Shift: The McKinsey Framework

McKinsey identifies a structural threat to legacy PSPs rooted in technology and economics:[^48][^35]

> *"New rails, stablecoins, and programmable money will transform the economics of consumer payments. Intelligent agents optimizing how and when consumers pay could put pressure on interchange revenues and yield spreads, straining the ability of local and regional players to grow and challenge the dominance of global companies."*

McKinsey's three strategic paths for financial institutions are:[^35]
- **Join** a multi-bank consortium stablecoin (preferred for large banks with network scale)
- **Partner** with a global issuer as distribution channel or custodian (preferred for mid-tier institutions)
- **Accept** deposit flight and lose payments revenue (default for institutions that do not act)

The BCG "Five Killer Tests" framework for evaluating stablecoin adoption in PSPs centers on whether the stablecoin solves structural payment inefficiencies: settlement time, cost, programmability, 24/7 availability, and cross-border reach. PSPs that do not develop stablecoin capabilities face "a narrow window" to build the operating and risk rails they still lack.[^42]
