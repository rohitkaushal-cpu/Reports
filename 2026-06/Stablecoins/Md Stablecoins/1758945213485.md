ABSTRACT

The  domestic  stablecoin  market  has  experienced  rapid
growth, with transaction volumes reaching KRW 57 trillion

during  the  first  quarter  of  2025.  In  response  to  ongoing

regulatory developments in major countries, active discus-

framework  for  stablecoins.  Currency-pegged  stablecoins

sive review of existing financial regulations, monetary poli-

Bitcoin, the leading crypto asset, was designed to operate

outside  government  control  within  the  crypto  ecosystem.

However,  its  high  price  volatility  has  limited  its  ability  to

function  as  a  stable  currency,  and  the  absence  of  an  ac-
countable  issuing  authority,  such  as  a  central  bank,  has

made it difficult to establish trust. As a result, the majority

of crypto assets, including Bitcoin, are primarily perceived

as  investment  assets,  whereas  currency-pegged  stable-
coins—designed  to  maintain  value  stability  and  redeema-

bility—have assumed the role of currency within the cryp-
to ecosystem1.

Stablecoins  have  taken  various  forms,  including  algorith-

mic types like Terra-Luna and asset-backed types pegged

to commodities such as precious metals. Yet, only curren-

cy-pegged stablecoins  are widely accepted for their value
stability2.  Given  their  close  links  with  traditional  fiat  cur-
rencies, currency-pegged stablecoins serve as the primary

bridge  between  traditional  fiat  currencies  and  crypto  as-

sets,  which  inevitably  raises  a  wide  range  of  regulatory
issues.

Key Considerations for Stablecoin Issuance

Regulatory  discussions  in  Korea  are  primarily  centered  on

permitting the issuance of stablecoins domestically, with a

particular  focus  on  institutionalizing  issuer  eligibility  and
issuance structures. Since the government banned domes-

tic  crypto  asset  issuance  in  2017,  all  crypto  assets—

including  stablecoins—have  been  issued  offshore.  Re-

newed  discussions  around  permitting  domestic  issuance
have therefore raised significant expectations among mar-

ket  participants.  However,  stablecoin  regulation  must  be

carefully  designed  to  account  for  the  various  regulatory

requirements previously outlined.

First,  regarding  issuer  eligibility,  it  is  essential  to  ensure

stable  and  continuous  business  operations,  as  well  as  the

issuer’s  credibility  and  public  trust—given  that  stablecoins
function not merely as investment assets, but as payment

and  settlement  instruments  equivalent  to  currency.  This

requires  more  than  simply  meeting  legal  entity  require-

ments; issuers must also adhere to comprehensive stand-
ards  related  to  governance  structures,  internal  controls,

and  the  qualifications  of  management,  in  line  with  their

business  plans.  For  example,  the  EU’s  MiCA  limits  stable-

coin (e-money tokens) issuers to credit institutions or elec-
tronic money institutions, while Japan restricts eligibility to

banks, trust companies, and funds transfer service provid-

ers  under  its  Payment  Services  Act.  In  the  case  of  the

U.S.,  the  GENIUS  Act  allows  various  types  of  issuers  but
subjects  them  to  strict  financial  regulatory  supervision.

Domestically,  issuers  of  electronic  money  or  prepaid  pay-

ment  instruments  are  required  to  meet  specific  personnel

should not be regarded as sufficient. Instead, issuers must
secure  adequate  capital  to  sustain  their  planned  business

operations  and  ensure  that  capital  levels  are  sufficient  to

meet  the  increasing  responsibilities  that  accompany  busi-

ness  growth.  For  instance,  the  EU  mandates  a  minimum
capital  of  €350,000  or  2%  of  the  stablecoin  issuance—

whichever  is  higher.  Similarly,  both  the  U.S.  and  Japan

require issuers to secure sufficient capital to carry out their
business plans effectively9.

Third,  funds  received  from  the  issuance  of  stablecoins

must be managed and operated as  reserves  composed of

high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to ensure they can meet
redemption demands. In the event of a sudden large-scale

redemption  (a  “coin  run”)  that  triggers  a  liquidity  crisis,

stablecoins cannot depend on central banks’ lender-of-last-

resort  functions  or  deposit  insurance  to  protect  users.
Therefore,  reserves  should  be  maintained  in  safe  and

highly liquid assets—such as demand deposits and govern-

ment  bonds—to  prepare  for  such  emergencies.  In  addi-

tion,  these  reserve  assets  should  be  managed  through
trust  structures  or  similar  arrangements  to  ensure  that

they  are  insulated  from  the  issuer’s  insolvency,  thereby

safeguarding user funds.

Fourth, it is important to consider the potential impact that

the accumulation and management of reserve assets may

have  on  related  asset  markets.  For  example,  demand  de-

posits held as reserves could trigger a bank run if a coin-
run spills over into traditional banks. In domestic markets

Designed to maintain a stable value pegged to a currency
and serve as a payment method comparable to traditional

money,  stablecoins  present  a  wide  range  of  regulatory

challenges—not  only  in  terms  of  their  institutional  frame-

work  but  also  in  their  trading  and  usage.  Therefore,  it  is
crucial  to  address  these  challenges  to  ensure  seamless

institutionalization of stablecoins.

with  existing  financial  regulations  under  the  principle  of

"same  function,  same  risk,  same  regulation”  to  prevent
regulatory  arbitrage  or  gaps12.  Current  financial  regula-

transactions  involving  fiat  currencies.  However,  overseas

markets  already  offer  services  involving  depositing  stable-

coins  to  receive  returns  similar  to  interest,  or  entrusting/
